The observation arrives in mixed units

The simulator's normaliser matches field names exactly against a 13-entry table and passes everything else through untouched. So zone temperature arrives as a z-score while the zone's own setpoints arrive in kelvin, fan pressure in pascals and occupancy as a head count. Each raw field type is listed below with what the encoder does with it.

Zone air temperature moves in minutes

The reward scores the zone's air temperature, and that air has little heat capacity. When an air handler switches on, a zone can swing several kelvin within one or two 5-minute steps, then fall back when it stops. The encoder keeps both a 5-minute and a 1-hour change so a model sees the spike and the trend.

Where the loss comes from

Loss per step splits into comfort, energy cost and carbon. Comfort is the largest part in every climate. In the cold, gas heating makes carbon the next largest. Comfort only counts while people are in, yet — of the energy and carbon loss comes while the building is empty, which a controller can cut without any comfort penalty.

    Which zones lose comfort

    Comfort is lost unevenly: in the median scenario the worst tenth of zones carries — of the loss. Facade exposure alone doesn't say which zones: facade-heavy zones lose most in most climates, but interior zones lose most in Atlanta, Denver and London. A controller that watches the average zone misses them, so the encoder gives each zone its own comfort shortfall and its distance to the worst zone on its air handler.

    Comfort penalty rebuilt per zone

    Did each choice help?

    Models predict a zone's temperature and its comfort penalty one hour ahead, scored on six unseen buildings in five unseen climates, twice: run by the best baseline, and run by varied controllers. The second matters most, because a controller built on the model will take actions the baseline would not.

    Two choices paid off. The varied data keeps improving as more of it is used while shipped-only data levels off: with 320,000 training rows the next-hour temperature R² on varied-run buildings is — against —. The second encoding helps the neural network, which depends on scaling and transforms: its comfort-penalty R² on varied-run buildings rises from — to —. Gradient boosting barely notices the encoding, as expected of trees. Predicting the effect of unusual actions is still the hard part: every model scores lower on varied-run buildings than on baseline-run ones.

    Method and terms
    Baseline
    The kit's best reference controller, "pid_water". Each scenario runs its first 7 days (2,016 steps), plus a copy of the shipped runs with exploration noise added to the actions. These are for analysis only: the leaderboard scores full 30-day episodes.
    Comfort shortfall
    Per zone: distance outside its heating/cooling band divided by 5 K, linear up to 1 and quadratic beyond, exactly as the reward computes it. The reward averages it over zones weighted by occupancy, which the controller cannot see, so the rebuilt total uses equal weights.
    Re-encoded features
    The audit sections near the top use the first encoding: 26 per zone and 16 building-wide. The current, second encoding is listed in full in the catalog: it adds tail transforms, gating by equipment state, physics interactions and longer history, and drops constant and duplicate columns. Both use fixed physical scales, so an unseen building cannot shift them, and both can be computed inside a submitted policy from the observation and building metadata.
    Varied collection
    Episodes of 3.5 days (the first eight ran 7 days) on all 40 generated train buildings, with weather windows from 20 train climates. Large buildings get two windows and the rest three, because simulation cost grows with floor area. The validation episodes with varied controllers use the six validation buildings and five validation climates.
    Raw observation
    For the same zone: its 6 fields, its air handler's 15, the hot-water loop's 7 and the 51 building-wide fields, as the environment delivers them.
    Models
    Ridge regression, a two-layer neural network (64 units each, early stopping) and gradient boosting, from scikit-learn, without per-feature scaling, so the comparison shows what each input looks like to the model as delivered.
